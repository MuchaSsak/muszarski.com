My old portfolio was a WebGL flight from Earth to Neptune. It looked great on my desktop. On a phone it showed an “Oops!” screen. This is the story of swapping it for a poster: the versions, the research, the sites I learned from and the reasons behind each call, mostly in pictures.

> **TL;DR**
>
> - **Before:** a 3D site that scored 34 on mobile Lighthouse and didn't work on phones.
> - **After:** Astro, three languages, one font, a few shaders that never get in the way, and 95 to 100 on mobile Lighthouse.
> - **How:** a project wiki before any code, research on award winners and real products, four live designs to pick from, and checks on every change.

## What rules did I work by?

Six rules: most written down before the first line of code, the rest learned on the way. Everything below follows from them.

1. **Plan before code.** The project lives in a wiki that every AI session reads first.
2. **Practical first, then wow.** Impressive within 3 seconds, usable in one tap on a phone.
3. **Research, then pick.** Real sites and real screens first; I choose between live options, not sketches.
4. **Show, don't tell.** A picture or a moving demo wherever a paragraph would go.
5. **Keep every version.** So I can show how it changed, and go back.
6. **Nothing ships unchecked.** Tests, screenshots I look at myself, speed on a slow phone.

## Why rebuild it?

Here is the old portfolio in motion:

<Clips
  clips={[{ src: "flight", dir: "/media/space/", width: 960, height: 540, label: "The old space portfolio: the camera flies past Mars to Jupiter", name: "The old site" }]}
  caption="The 3D space portfolio, recorded on a desktop. Planet textures: Solar System Scope, CC BY 4.0."
/>

Beautiful on my desktop. Then I measured it, on 5 October 2026:

- **13.8 MB before the start button.** 7.6 MB of it was an emoji font.
- **34 on mobile Lighthouse.** On a throttled phone the main content took 46.9 seconds to appear.
- **Phones got an “Oops!” screen.** The site didn't run there at all.
- **Only a mouse wheel moved it.** No touch swipe, no keyboard.
- **Nothing for search engines.** The raw HTML had no text to index.

Someone opening a portfolio from a LinkedIn message on a phone gives it maybe ten seconds. My old site spent them on a loading bar. It proved I can write shaders; it also proved I hadn't thought about the person on the other side.

So no preloader, no gate, no scroll-jacking, and the same content on every screen.

## What is a portfolio for?

Showing your work. It sounds obvious, yet many portfolios bury the visitor under “about me” paragraphs, or pitch their tech stack as if it were sacred, and the projects end up three scrolls down. Here the projects come right after the name: “See the work” is the first button, the tools are one strip of logos, and the about-me has a page of its own.

What came out of it:

- **An award doesn't mean usable.** On the throttled phone only 8 of the 20 were usable within 3 seconds, and 9 weren't usable within 10. The four least practical (game worlds and gated intros) even had a higher average Site of the Day score than the three most practical ones.
- **The jury isn't hiring.** Awwwards weighs design 40%, usability 30%, creativity 20% and content 10%, and the jurors are designers and developers. A recruiter asks one thing: can I find the projects and the contact?
- **Stunning and practical is possible, but rare.** Only four sites scored 4 or more on both. What they share: real links on the first screen, and the show beside the content, never in front of it.

## Why not another 3D world?

Honestly, I love them. My last portfolio was one. But I had measured both kinds of site by then, and the trade looked like this:

| | A full 3D world | This site |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First screen | a loader or “click to start” | my name, my role and two buttons |
| Links | often none in the HTML (Samsy, Jordan Delcros) | every link is real HTML |
| Search engines and screen readers | text drawn in WebGL is invisible to them | real text, painted first |
| Slow phone | 9 of 20 award winners unusable within 10 s | shaders start after the text, only while visible |
| No WebGL or WebGPU | an error instead of the site (Kris Temmerman) | a CSS picture underneath |

So the wow stays, as a layer instead of a gate. Four shaders run on this site, all written for it with no library:

## How do I run the AI?

Like a small team. I brief the lead agent, subagents research in parallel, MCP servers bring real data, hooks guard the risky commands, checks gate the result, and I review it. The AI does a lot of the typing; I own the decisions.

## Who else is it built for?

Everyone, including people who use the web differently. A site that only works with a mouse and perfect eyesight shuts people out, and I'd rather it didn't. So:

- **Motion you can stop.** One “Pause motion” button stops everything, and with reduced motion turned on, everything shows its final state ([more](/en/blog/reduced-motion-and-a-pause-button/)).
- **A keyboard gets everywhere.** A skip link jumps past the header, every hover has a focus twin, and focus never hides under the sticky header.
- **Screen readers hear the full story.** Hidden text adds what the eye gets from context, like “opens in a new tab”, and every button's name starts with the words you see on it.
- **Contrast is tested, not eyeballed.** A unit test checks every colour pair, and axe runs on every page.

## How do search engines know who I am?

Sitemaps and meta tags are the floor. To find me by name, and one day show a knowledge panel, a search engine has to be sure who the site is about. So every page tells it the same story:

- **One person, one id.** The home page and the CV describe me as one `Person` in structured data: my name, my role, my real portrait and my profiles. Every post and My life point back to it, and the links to my profiles carry `rel="me"`.
- **Real photos with real facts.** My life shows my own photos, each one marked up with where and when it was taken, and listed in the image sitemap.
- **A named author.** Every post is a `BlogPosting` by that same person, with an author box under it, and every outside fact has a dated source.
- **A footer that does the work.** Every page links to the projects, the CV, the blog, its RSS feed and my profiles.
- **One address per page.** One canonical URL, the language versions linked to each other, and a share card for every page in every language.

The build fails if a page loses its title, description or canonical, or if the structured data stops parsing.
