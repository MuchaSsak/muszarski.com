I built Linkoglot, an AI language-learning app, alone in a few months, and shipped it to Google Play on 10 July 2026 and to the App Store on 21 July 2026. One person can cover two platforms because every heavy job has a tool behind it: Expo for the native apps, Supabase for the backend, RevenueCat for subscriptions on both stores, Lingui for 30 languages. What stays with you is scope, the store requirements, and deciding which rules the phone is never trusted with.

> **TL;DR**
>
> - Alone means every layer: the app, the database, the AI prompts, payments, store listings, a landing page, legal documents and account deletion.
> - Expo generates and builds both native apps from one codebase, RevenueCat puts one SDK over both stores' billing, and Postgres enforces the rules a client could fake.
> - Both stores ask for account deletion, privacy disclosures and store billing for digital features. Plan them as features in week one, not as paperwork in the last week.

## What did "alone" include?

Linkoglot turns what is around you into study material. You point the camera at a scene, a page of text or a few objects (the camera has Landscape, Text and Items modes), and AI turns the photo into flashcards, quizzes and challenges. Then you practise: text chats with an AI tutor, pronunciation, and real-time voice conversations.

Building it alone meant every layer, not only the app:

- the product and its design;
- the Expo app for iPhone and Android;
- the Supabase schema, row-level security and triggers;
- the AI prompts;
- payments: a free trial, then a monthly or yearly subscription;
- both store listings, with translated text and screenshots per language;
- a Next.js landing page in 30 locales, the privacy policy, the terms and a web page for deleting your account;
- a spin-off, the npm CLI `lingui-translate-ai`.

I wrote a lot of it by hand at first, and later a lot with AI. Either way, each decision below was mine to make and mine to fix.

## Which jobs does the stack carry?

| Job | Tool | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Native iOS and Android projects | Expo, with a development build and prebuild | Generates the `ios` and `android` folders from the app config and config plugins |
| Building and signing | EAS Build | A hosted build service; iOS builds run on Expo's macOS runners, and it can manage keystores, provisioning profiles and certificates |
| Accounts, data, photos, live updates | Supabase | Auth with Google and Apple sign-in, Postgres, Storage, Realtime |
| Server data on the phone | TanStack Query | A client cache that the app persists between launches |
| Subscriptions on both stores | RevenueCat | One SDK over StoreKit and Google Play Billing |
| 30 languages | Lingui | Messages in the code, a `.po` catalog per language |

The UI is NativeWind with React Native Reusables.

Expo calls this Continuous Native Generation: "short-lived native projects are generated only when needed". Manual edits to those folders can be lost on the next `npx expo prebuild --clean`, so changes belong in config plugins.

Linkoglot needs one native piece outside the main app: an iOS Notification Service Extension for push notifications. It comes from `@bacons/apple-targets`, a config plugin that generates Apple targets during prebuild from a `targets/` folder. So even the extension is generated, not edited by hand into an Xcode project.

The development build (the app compiled with `expo-dev-client`, in Expo's words) matters for payments too. RevenueCat's Expo guide says that inside Expo Go the SDK swaps native calls for mock APIs, and "real purchases will not function in this mode".

## What should the database decide, not the phone?

A phone app is a client you don't control. Two parts of Linkoglot don't depend on the phone telling the truth.

**Achievements.** XP, achievements and day streaks are the game layer. Achievements are awarded by `AFTER INSERT` and `AFTER UPDATE` triggers in Postgres, which call a function in a `private` schema. The achievements table has row-level security on, with every client operation disabled. Supabase's docs state the effect: "Once RLS is enabled, no data is accessible through the API when using a publishable key, until you create policies." The app can't grant itself an achievement, and the rule is written once for both platforms.

**Photos.** Before a photo leaves the phone, the app resizes it to 1280 px tall and saves it as a JPEG at quality 0.25. It goes to Supabase Storage under the user's own folder, the analysis runs on the server, and the app reads the result back. Only a compressed picture travels to Storage and to the model.

## What do the stores ask for before you ship?

Store rules turn into screens, forms and code, so they belong in the plan. These four shaped Linkoglot:

| Requirement | Rule | In Linkoglot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delete your account | Apple 5.1.1(v): an app with account creation must offer deletion in the app. Google Play: an in-app path plus a web link for deletion requests | Deletion in the app, and a Delete Account page on the website |
| A private login option | Apple 4.8: an app with Google Sign-In or another social login must also offer an equivalent login that limits data to name and email, lets people keep their email private and doesn't collect app interactions for ads without consent | Sign in with Google and with Apple |
| Privacy disclosures | Apple's privacy details, which include data your third-party SDKs collect. Google Play's Data safety form, required even on closed and open testing tracks | The App Store lists Purchases, Contact Info, User Content, Identifiers, Usage Data and Diagnostics as linked to you. Play states that data is encrypted in transit and that you can request deletion |
| Store billing for digital features | Apple 3.1.1: unlocking features needs in-app purchase. Google Play: in-app features and digital content go through Google Play's billing system, with listed exceptions | RevenueCat over both billing systems |

Account deletion shows how one rule becomes two features: a screen in the app, and a page on the web, because Play also asks for a link where people can request deletion.

## What did the first update fix?

Version 1.0.1 reached Google Play on 22 July and the App Store on 29 July. The release note: "Fixed a bug where study sets generated with AI in the background were saved correctly but didn't refresh on screen."

The data was right and the screen was stale. With a client cache like TanStack Query, a screen shows cached data until something marks it out of date. TanStack's docs describe `invalidateQueries` for the case "when you know for a fact that a query's data is out of date": it marks queries stale and refetches the ones on screen. A background job that writes data has a second job: telling the screen.

## How do subscriptions work across two stores?

The model is a free download, a free trial, then a monthly or yearly subscription. The App Store text says: "A monthly or yearly subscription unlocks full access to all features."

RevenueCat describes its product as "a backend and SDKs that wrap StoreKit, Google Play Billing, and RevenueCat Billing". In the app, that's the `react-native-purchases` SDK behind my own hooks for setup, purchase, cancellation and currency conversion. The access check RevenueCat documents is short:

```ts
const customerInfo = await Purchases.getCustomerInfo();
// "pro" stands for your own entitlement identifier
const hasAccess = typeof customerInfo.entitlements.active["pro"] !== "undefined";
```

An entitlement, in RevenueCat's words, represents "a level of access, features, or content that a user is 'entitled' to". The SDK caches customer info and refreshes the cache when it is older than 5 minutes, but only on `getCustomerInfo()`, a purchase or a restore. For changes in between, `addCustomerInfoUpdateListener` fires whenever the info changes on the device.

**Credits on top of the subscription.** AI features are metered in credits. The monthly plan includes 10,000 credits a month and the annual plan 10,500. Extra credits never expire and are only used after the monthly ones run out. The published cost table (dated 7 June 2026) shows how far apart the features are:

| Feature | Recommended model | Max model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flashcards, base generation | 0.88 | 2.4 |
| Chat, per message | 1.7 | 5.52 |
| Voice conversation, per minute | 100 | 100 |

On the recommended model, one minute of voice costs as many credits as about 59 chat messages.

## How does one person ship 30 languages?

The App Store lists 30 languages for Linkoglot, and the landing page has the same 30 locales, each with an hreflang alternate. Text lives in four places, and each one has its own route:

1. **App strings.** Lingui, with a `.po` catalog per language, the same library this site uses ([how this site was built](/en/blog/how-this-site-was-built/)). [Lingui vs i18next](/en/blog/lingui-vs-i18next/) compares the two libraries.
2. **Translation at scale.** An in-repo script sent catalogs to an LLM in batches. On 17 June 2026 it became the npm CLI `lingui-translate-ai`, and version 1.1.0, with ICU plural support, came out on 10 July 2026, the day the Play release went live. [How the tool works, and how I check its drafts](/en/blog/translate-lingui-catalogs-with-an-llm/).
3. **Database content.** Achievement names and descriptions are stored as jsonb, and a `translate_column` trigger fills in the translations.
4. **Store listings.** Translated listing text and screenshots per language.

One check if you do the same: Lingui's React Native guide notes that `Intl` support "can vary across React Native and OS versions" and polyfills `Intl.Locale` and `Intl.PluralRules`. Linkoglot's minimums are iOS 16.4 and Android 7.0, so plurals are worth testing on the oldest versions you support.
